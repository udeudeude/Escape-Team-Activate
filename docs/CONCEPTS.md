# Escape Team Activate: Concept slate (2026-10-20)

Status: preproduction concepts, **not playtested**. These are original premises and speculative mechanisms, not existing Escape Team missions. No puzzles from the publisher's paid missions will be copied.

## Shared constraints

Official Mission Editor: up to five ordered phases, exactly one five-digit numeric answer per phase, up to eight hints per phase, optional MP3 at introduction, phase completion (1–4), victory and timeout, linked external printable files, configurable countdown. Hints depend on elapsed phase time and/or wrong attempts; precise scheduling not yet tested. App does not document programmable branches, arbitrary event triggers or additional controls. Player can choose "keep trying" after time expires, then timer counts up. Primary source: https://www.escape-team.com/create

**Design principles**
1. Codes verify accomplishments; they should rarely be the only things players are trying to discover.
2. Each phase offers a reveal/change in understanding.
3. Multiple simultaneous tasks converge, so all 2–4 players have agency.
4. Puzzles must be fairly clued, with progressive non-spoiling hints and fully reasoned solutions.
5. Audio is atmospheric or duplicatively captioned unless explicitly designated as essential with accessibility alternative.
6. Normal paper, scissors, tape, pencils and a phone must suffice in the default print-and-play edition.
7. Blind playtest with people who have not seen the designs before claiming balanced difficulty.

## Concept A: THE LAST BROADCAST

**Genre:** uncanny archival mystery. **Players:** 2–4. **Goal duration:** 45–55 minutes. **Emotional arc:** curiosity -> unease -> discovery -> agency.

**Pitch:** The same eleven-minute recording has been transmitted nightly by an abandoned radio station since 1973. Tonight it changes, apparently addressing the investigators. Players are asked to identify the source of the signal. Evidence reveals that the numbered broadcasts are *replies*, not transmissions, and that someone is depending on the players to answer.

**Mechanically distinctive:** Sound functions as contested evidence. Players compare timing diagrams, handwritten logs, spoken transcripts and physical paper dials. A piece of evidence that appeared to provide an answer instead reveals that a presumed signal direction was inverted. A transmission after a phase-completion code is a diegetic witness, not applause.

**Five app phases (code checkpoints):**
1. **Restore the log.** Two or three teams independently rebuild a broadcast schedule from clipped records and a station clock. Their consistency yields a log identifier (five digits). New message: the voice on the tape predates the supposed recording.
2. **Trace the signal.** Slide a paper tuning strip across a circuit map; logs and a separate hand-drawn frequency table establish correct orientation and yield a route identifier. Audible clues have printed equivalents.
3. **Identify the speaker.** Reconcile mutually inconsistent archived statements and a room diagram. Players realize that the name everyone is using is a role, not a person.
4. **Reverse the model.** Revisit the signal map with transmitter and receiver exchanged; previously inexplicable marks now form a coherent pattern. Earlier evidence becomes useful again.
5. **Choose what to broadcast.** Players physically assemble a message and identify a final sequence. The last code records a consequential act, not merely another solved cipher. An optional paper epilogue could differ based on a prior choice, although the app's ending remains linear.

**Prototype (10–15 min):** One two-sided "maintenance circular" cut into a sliding calibration strip; one logging sheet listing five signal interruptions; one dispatcher's note that leads solvers to flip the apparatus. First solve appears to point at a transmitter; a second alignment exposes a receiver. Code comes from the revealed station register number. Provide captioned text for all audio.

**Risks:** audio quality/mute switch; underclued reversal; making the twist feel arbitrary; too much reliance on ciphers instead of deduction. **Test:** Did players independently articulate the reversed model *before* reading the confirmation?

## Concept B: THE HOUSE THAT WASN'T THERE

**Genre:** architectural / forensic spatial mystery. **Players:** 2–4. **Goal duration:** 45–60 minutes. **Emotional arc:** investigation -> mastery -> spatial impossibility -> discovery.

**Pitch:** A municipal building-survey team must document a condemned house before demolition, but the floor plans disagree: the building appears to contain a room with no exterior access, and each survey locates it elsewhere. Someone has deliberately altered the records to hide an occupant, an event, or a route.

**Mechanically distinctive:** The paper *is* the environment. Different sheets represent competing maps from different years or viewpoints. Cutouts, fold-up walls, a paper light mask, and overlays transform the evidence. Players do not merely read maps: they reconstruct a space and find the error in the surveys. Unlike a fixed 3-D model, all needed information stays printable.

**Five app phases:**
1. **Admit the survey.** Combine four independent inspection annotations to establish which plan is authoritative at the entrance; enter its five-digit filing number.
2. **Construct the interior.** Fold a simple wall-and-stair net and reconcile elevation and plan drawings. A hidden number is revealed only after correct construction.
3. **Follow the absence.** Each player follows a separate witness's recorded route on a different dated plan; routes become consistent when two marked locations are swapped.
4. **Expose the false wall.** Overlay a street survey on the corrected floor plans. The purported extra room occupies a space incorrectly labeled in every official record. The foldable building exposes access.
5. **Recover the record.** The final physical act reveals what was concealed, and five digits verify its archive designation. Reveal answers *why* the record was altered, not just where it went.

**Prototype:** Two small building plans, one movable window mask, one route testimony, and one foldable wall/stair profile. Players discover that a corridor labeled north in one plan actually points south because its compass rose is on the reverse of a reused paper sheet. Then they correct the plan and identify a room index. This must be tested for fairness and for visibility on black-and-white home printers.

**Risks:** print scaling and alignment, delicate assembly, color reliance, spatial puzzle causing one player to monopolize the group. **Test:** Can two people manipulate the artifact while others still make independent meaningful deductions?

## Concept C: THE MINISTRY OF MISPLACED HOURS

**Genre:** eerie bureaucratic fantasy / collaborative investigation. **Players:** 2–5. **Goal duration:** 40–55 minutes. **Emotional arc:** amusement -> procedural confidence -> doubt -> moral discovery.

**Pitch:** Players are temporary clerks at an agency that records hours "misplaced" by citizens. A backlog threatens automatic deletion of records. The task seems to be finding fraudulent claims. Forms and contradictory regulations instead reveal that the agency deliberately misclassifies genuine experiences as errors. The players must decide what to preserve.

**Mechanically distinctive:** Instructions themselves are the puzzle surface. The fun comes not from decoding a fictional alphabet but from reading conflicting documents, checking precedence, placing physical slips into files, and discovering that a regulatory *exception* changes the task. Designed to distribute information among players: no individual starts with all the rules.

**Five app phases:**
1. **Open the case.** Assign each player one type of record (claim, register, audit, regulations); reconcile the filing number.
2. **Enforce the rule.** Sort time slips under apparently straightforward regulations. Correct sorting requires discovering a priority clause that overrides a later printed instruction.
3. **Audit the clock.** Place paper clock hands and produce an apparent deficit. Cross-check the ledger, revealing that a "missing" hour was deliberately reassigned.
4. **Find the beneficiary.** Players must cross-reference the names suppressed from different records. No single sheet contains the full identity.
5. **Make the filing.** The group must decide which legitimate historical account to preserve. A physical filing mechanism produces the final code, and the paper epilogue reflects the decision (the app can validate a common code if paths reconverge).

**Prototype:** Four short documents: a claims slip, time register, regulations, and an auditor's annotation. The regulations contain a clear but easy-to-overlook hierarchy: exceptions printed under a signed corrigendum override the main sorting rule. Sorting changes when the hierarchy is correctly applied. One resulting docket number becomes a five-digit checkpoint. Avoid creating a mere trick question: all regulations remain publicly discoverable, and contradiction is resolved by an explicit rule.

**Risks:** too much reading, semantics masquerading as puzzles, too much mandatory discussion, a false sense of choice if the ending only offers a single outcome. **Test:** Do all players contribute, and does the exception feel earned rather than like a gotcha?

## Comparative evaluation

| Criterion | Broadcast | House | Ministry |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strongest dimension | Narrative recontextualization | Physical transformation | Procedural/social novelty |
| Reliance on phone audio | Medium, but optional | Low | Low |
| Printer precision risk | Moderate | High | Low |
| Risk of text overload | Medium | Low | High |
| Branching opportunity | Medium | Low–medium | High (physical only) |
| Remote play adaptability | Medium | Low | Medium–high |
| Best 10–15-minute prototype | Receiver/transmitter inversion | Correcting survey map | Regulation priority exception |

**Current recommendation:** Prototype **The House That Wasn't There** first as an engineering stress-test for printable physical interaction, while treating **The Last Broadcast** as the more promising 45–60-minute flagship. The Ministry provides a different axis for testing player collaboration and choices. Do not select purely from premises; build and blind-test a significant mechanism.

## Testing gates

- Mechanism audit: unique intended solution, no extraneous inferred rules, no easy shortcut.
- Physical audit: US Letter/A4 compatibility, monochrome printing, 100% scale and scaling tolerance, minimum readable fonts, cut line clarity, no need for household specialty items.
- Accessibility: do not use color alone; provide text equivalents to sound; avoid mandatory split-second physical or audio cues.
- App audit: wrong-answer feedback and penalties, 0-leading solutions, audio playback URLs, hints, timeout continuation, distribution and edit updates. Requires on-device testing.
- Blind test: observe without leading questions, measure phase times, note stalls, collect qualitative descriptions of each reveal and how fun it was.
